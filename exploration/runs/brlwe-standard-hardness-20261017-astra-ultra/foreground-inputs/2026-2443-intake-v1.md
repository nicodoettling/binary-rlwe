## What the paper supplies, and what remains an assumption

SuperSVP supplies a basis of a lattice Λ and a short basis P of a superlattice Λ′ containing Λ^⊕r. Its promises are maximum column length at most β and index |Λ′/Λ^⊕r| at most 2^k. The requested output is a nonzero vector in Λ of length at most γ′ (Definition 4.1, p.31). This is an absolute output bound, not an automatically specified approximation factor times λ₁. The paper uses ℓ for r; that is unrelated to our source-noise width ceil(log₂ n).

The central reduction is SuperSVP → succinct SIS (Theorem 4.2); there is a reverse reduction with changed dimensions and parameters (Theorem 4.10). A search succinct-LWE solver gives a quantum succinct-SIS solver (Theorem 7.1). Succinct/decomposed LWE are related by transformations preserving the secret law; decomposed search-to-decision additionally assumes ordinary decisional LWE in stated parameters (Theorem 6.1). Keep these dependencies separate when composing a chain. Neither ordinary SVP nor standard RLWE is shown to imply all the application-relevant SuperSVP regimes.

The parameter table on pp.32–33 matters: the reduction is supplied σ₀ ≥ 2η_{2^{-(λ+2)}}(Λ), and returns length γ′ = 4γγ_det σ₀, where γ is the succinct-SIS solution bound. Its modulus and rounding scales depend on the input basis, its inverse, the hint quality, and σ₀. Polynomial encoding length does not by itself put this in our desired fixed polynomial-modulus family. Do not compose the informal arrows before checking a simultaneous parameter regime.

The paper itself gives two useful sanity checks (§1.3): a sufficiently permissive index bound lets one manufacture a short superlattice basis from any input basis, recovering ordinary SVP; conversely, multiplying a hint vector by the quotient index immediately produces a vector in the target lattice of length at most 2^k β. More sharply, the quotient exponent can replace its cardinality in this elementary argument. Thus plausibility depends on the *joint* hint/index/output regime. Worst-case terminology alone does not settle it, and the six-day-old preprint does not yet provide a long record of independent scrutiny.

## Highest-priority import: geometry of a small quotient

The constructive idea in §§2.1, 3.5 and 4 is to round using a short superlattice basis, retain the resulting quotient class explicitly, and encode that class with short coefficients. Smith normal form gives Γ ≅ ⊕ Z/d_j Z. A binary gadget uses sum_j ceil(log₂ d_j) columns, at most twice log₂|Γ|. The quotient representative need not already have integral coordinates in the desired lattice scale; the reduction rounds it and pays for the residual. It then masks the quotient data and samples a canonical Gaussian trapdoor. This is a useful design pattern for our finite-quotient bottleneck, although Smith normal form, gadgets and Gaussian sampling are established tools.

There is a precise preliminary correspondence worth testing. Identify coefficient vectors with Z^n. Let I_T ⊂ R_q be our d-dimensional CRT ideal and let

    J_T = {z ∈ Z^n : z mod q ∈ I_T}.

Then qZ^n ⊂ J_T ⊂ Z^n, with [Z^n:J_T] = q^(n-d). For the Euclidean coefficient dual, set K_T = q J_T*. We obtain

    qZ^n ⊂ K_T ⊂ Z^n,       [Z^n:K_T] = q^d,
    K_T mod q = I_T^⊥.

Proof of the last identity: x ∈ qJ_T* exactly when x has integer coefficients and ⟨x,z⟩ is divisible by q for every z ∈ J_T. The dimension and index follow from the nondegenerate coefficient pairing. This dual orientation exposes the *same* d log₂q quantity that controls our binary-image leakage; the primal quotient instead has (n-d) log₂q bits. For power-of-two cyclotomics, translating this to the canonical/trace dual requires its scaling and involution explicitly.

Consequently, K_T with superlattice Z^n is a natural restricted SuperSVP-style instance: a unit-column hint, quotient cardinality q^d, and exactly d invariant factors equal to q. Its quotient gadget has d ceil(log₂ q) columns. This is only a geometric identification. The random split-ideal family is a special family, whereas the theorem concerns worst-case general lattice pairs. It does not establish hardness for these K_T, nor a reduction from their short-vector problem to our two noisy ideal samples. T is public in the known-lattice formulation; a hidden-source reduction still needs both complete challenge laws.

First falsification control: this quotient has exponent q, so q times a unit vector is already in K_T. A proposed worst-case guarantee γ′ ≥ q is vacuous for this normalization. Test whether a candidate reduction has γ′ < q while meeting all smoothing, gadget and target-noise inequalities. Also track more efficient algorithms from the ideal structure. Merely showing a small quotient is not evidence of hardness.

## Second import: randomize while preserving short inverse maps

Theorem 5.1 and Lemma 5.8 use a unitriangular matrix

    S = [[I, X], [0, I]],     S^-1 = [[I, -X], [0, I]],
    [A₀ | A_rest] S = [A₀ | A₀X + A_rest].

This allows simultaneous transport of the LWE response and the public trapdoor, with controlled norms. Canonical Gaussian resampling repairs the transported trapdoor's distribution, and noise rerandomization repairs the transformed error law. The proof handles correlations between A₀ and A_rest; marginal uniformity of A₀ is not enough to replace it independently. The authors credit the unitriangular technique to Micciancio–Peikert and related subsequent work.

This suggests a concrete public-map test for our current R1/R2 efforts: can a ring/module-compatible triangular change of coordinates preserve the response, source structure and a usable short inverse, while giving the desired joint uniform comparator? The algebraic inverse exists over a ring, but the paper's general-matrix leftover-hash argument does not transfer automatically to multiplication matrices. At each nontrivial ring character/CRT component, establish the required joint extraction with all public information retained. Preserve the noise budget and target rank-one interpretation. This alone does not evade our pure two-seed ideal-preserving expansion obstruction.

## Third import: make the extra information part of the experiment

The hinted-LWE framework publishes (A,W,T), where T is a short gadget preimage for [I_r ⊗ A | W]. Instead of treating its availability as a hidden trapdoor for the reduction, the paper explicitly prices the information in the computational problem. Its Gaussian resampling steps distinguish a short witness from a witness with the required conditional distribution.

For us, an ideal-compatible hinted intermediate could be a better-defined alternative to bare hidden-ideal pseudorandomness, especially for the current Gaussian-periodic and denominator routes. Specify exactly who receives the ideal, basis, short relations and hints. Then ask whether those hints can be simulated from an established-hardness input and whether their presence still permits the binary recovery reduction. If the intermediate needs a new SuperSVP-style premise, record that as a separate conditional result, not as achievement of the running standard-hardness goal. Do not import the hidden ideal as free advice or infer average-case hardness from hardness for arbitrary known lattices.

## Useful subsidiary sections, with limits

- Lemma 8.4 extends samples while retaining correlated hints. It provides a comparison point for EX-v2; it is not another reason to redo our already completed conditional sample expansion.
- Lemma 8.6 gives a *discrete-Gaussian* small secret, not a binary secret. Its normal-form transformation resamples the hint to the changed public coordinates.
- Theorem 5.1 and Lemma 8.8 preserve an arbitrary supplied secret sampler. Choosing that sampler to be binary only transports hardness that must already be established for that sampler; it does not derive binary hardness from uniform-secret hardness.
- Lemma 8.8 permits a shift F depending on W and an auxiliary public M, but not on the fresh short matrix R. Trying to choose F to cancel W(I⊗R) into a desired ring matrix violates that condition. Check this before any “programmable shift” proposal.
- §8.4 handles a fixed public prefix and an inherited secret distribution. It is not a binary-secret theorem.
- Generic dense matrices cannot be replaced by ring multiplication matrices, or conditioned to become such matrices, without a new reduction and its distributional/conditioning costs.
