## 1. Frozen meaning and quantifiers

Let n tend to infinity through powers of two. Set

\[
K=K_n=\mathbb Q(\zeta_{2n}),\qquad R=\mathcal O_K,
\]

where the root of unity is primitive. An ideal in the count is a nonzero integral ideal of R, with absolute norm

\[
N(J)=[R:J].
\]

The class group is the ordinary group of invertible fractional ideals modulo principal fractional ideals. Let h_K be its order. For a fixed real c>0, define

\[
C_n(c)=\{[I]:\text{some integral }J\in[I]\text{ has }N(J)\le e^{cn}\}.
\]

A precise reading of the claimed estimate is

\[
\limsup_{\substack{n\to\infty\\n\text{ a power of }2}}
\frac{\log(|C_n(c)|/h_K)}{n\log n}\le-\frac14.
\tag{T}
\]

Equivalently, for every fixed c>0 and every epsilon>0, there is a threshold N(c,epsilon) such that all powers of two n beyond it satisfy

\[
\frac{|C_n(c)|}{h_K}\le
\exp\bigl(-(1/4-\epsilon)n\log n\bigr).
\]

The threshold is not supplied effectively. The small-o term need not have a prescribed sign. The assertion is an upper bound, not a matching asymptotic equality for the fraction. No uniformity over c growing with n is claimed.

## 2. Degrees, signatures, and exact discriminants

Write n=2^k with k>=1, put d=n/2, and abbreviate zeta=zeta_(2n). The cyclotomic polynomial is

\[
\Phi_{2n}(X)=X^n+1.
\]

It follows that [K:Q]=n. The field K is Galois and abelian over Q. For n>=2 its signature is (0,d). Its maximal real subfield

\[
F=\mathbb Q(\zeta+\zeta^{-1})
\]

has degree d, signature (d,0), and is Galois over Q: its subgroup in the abelian Galois group of K is generated by complex conjugation.

The cyclotomic integer-ring theorem gives O_K=Z[zeta]. In this particular family, it also follows from an elementary local argument. The polynomial (X+1)^n+1 is Eisenstein at 2, and zeta-1 is a uniformizer in the degree-n extension of Q_2. In a power-basis expansion over Q_2, the valuations of different terms are distinct modulo n, so integrality forces all coefficients into Z_2. At odd primes the discriminant of X^n+1 has no prime divisor, hence there is no possible index. These observations give the full global integer ring.

Taking the norm of the derivative n*zeta^(n-1) gives

\[
D_K:=|\Delta_K|=n^n.
\tag{D1}
\]

For completeness, the real-field discriminant can be derived without assuming a tabulated formula. Let t=zeta+zeta^(-1). In the integer basis 1,zeta,...,zeta^(n-1), complex conjugation maps zeta^j to -zeta^(n-j) for 1<=j<n. Its fixed elements therefore have the form

\[
a_0+\sum_{j=1}^{d-1}a_j(\zeta^j+\zeta^{-j}),\qquad a_j\in\mathbb Z.
\]

The sums zeta^j+zeta^(-j) are integer polynomials in t, by the recurrence P_(j+1)=t P_j-P_(j-1). Thus O_F=Z[t]. Also

\[
\mathcal O_K=\mathcal O_F\oplus\mathcal O_F\zeta,
\]

because zeta satisfies X^2-tX+1 and 1,zeta are F-linearly independent. The relative discriminant ideal is consequently generated by

\[
t^2-4=(\zeta-\zeta^{-1})^2.
\]

Its absolute norm is 4. Indeed, zeta^2 is a primitive n-th root, [K:Q(zeta^2)]=2, and Phi_n(1)=2, including n=2. Therefore

\[
|N_{K/\mathbb Q}(\zeta-\zeta^{-1})|
=|N_{K/\mathbb Q}(1-\zeta^2)|=2^2=4.
\]

The absolute value of this norm is also the absolute norm from F of (zeta-zeta^(-1))^2. Discriminant transitivity now yields

\[
D_K=D_F^2\cdot4,\qquad
D_F:=|\Delta_F|=\frac{n^{n/2}}2.
\tag{D2}
\]

In particular,

\[
\log D_K=n\log n,\qquad
\log D_F=\frac n2\log n-\log2.
\tag{D3}
\]

All factors of 2 here are exact. The asymptotic arguments below start at n>=4, so the exceptional zero denominator log(D_F)=0 at n=2 causes no problem.

## 3. Applicability of normal-field Brauer–Siegel

The imported analytic theorem is precisely this: along normal number fields L for which [L:Q]/log|Delta_L| tends to zero,

\[
\log(h_L\operatorname{Reg}_L)
=\frac12\log|\Delta_L|+o(\log|\Delta_L|).
\tag{BS}
\]

Both sequences here satisfy its hypotheses. They are normal, and

\[
\frac{[K:\mathbb Q]}{\log D_K}=\frac1{\log n}\longrightarrow0,
\]

\[
\frac{[F:\mathbb Q]}{\log D_F}
=\frac{n/2}{(n/2)\log n-\log2}\longrightarrow0.
\]

Their discriminants tend to infinity. There is no fixed-degree requirement in the frozen version of (BS). Applying it separately to K and F gives

\[
A_n:=\log(h_K\operatorname{Reg}_K)
=\frac12 n\log n+o(n\log n),
\tag{B1}
\]

\[
B_n:=\log(h_F\operatorname{Reg}_F)
=\frac14 n\log n+o(n\log n).
\tag{B2}
\]

Both error terms are small relative to the same scale n log n. Their difference is therefore also o(n log n). Brauer–Siegel alone controls h times the regulator; it does not justify replacing h times the regulator by h.

## 4. Regulator comparison and class-number lower bound

Use the usual regulator convention with log absolute values at real embeddings and twice log absolute values at complex embeddings. A deleted-row determinant defines the regulator; rank-zero regulators are 1.

Both K and F have unit rank d-1. Let E_K and E_F be their unit groups, and mu_K the finite root-of-unity group of K. Define

\[
Q=[E_K:\mu_KE_F].
\]

Dirichlet's unit theorem shows Q is finite. The log vector of a real unit, when regarded as a K-unit, is twice its F log vector. Comparing full-rank lattices gives the exact formula

\[
\operatorname{Reg}_K
=\frac{2^{d-1}}Q\operatorname{Reg}_F.
\tag{R1}
\]

For this proof it is already enough to use Q>=1 and therefore Reg_K<=2^(d-1) Reg_F. Nevertheless, the usual bound Q<=2 can be checked directly, removing any uncertainty about the relative asymptotic below.

For u in E_K, the unit u/conjugate(u) has all conjugates of absolute value 1, hence is a root of unity by Kronecker's lemma. One quick proof of that lemma is that the monic integer polynomials of all powers of such an algebraic integer have bounded degree and bounded coefficients; there are only finitely many possible roots among those polynomials, so two powers coincide. The map u -> u/conjugate(u) induces an injection

\[
E_K/(\mu_KE_F)\hookrightarrow\mu_K/\mu_K^2.
\]

Indeed, if u/conjugate(u)=xi^2 for xi in mu_K, then u/xi is fixed by conjugation and is a unit of F. The finite root-of-unity group is cyclic and contains -1, so its quotient by squares has order 2. Thus Q is either 1 or 2. No exact value of Q for this family is required.

Combining (B1), (B2), and (R1),

\[
\begin{aligned}
\log(h_K/h_F)
&=A_n-B_n-\log(\operatorname{Reg}_K/\operatorname{Reg}_F)\\
&=\frac14 n\log n+o(n\log n)
 -(d-1)\log2+\log Q\\
&=\frac14 n\log n+o(n\log n).
\end{aligned}
\tag{H1}
\]

Since h_F is a positive integer,

\[
\log h_K\ge\frac14 n\log n+o(n\log n).
\tag{H2}
\]

Equivalently, h_K>=exp((1/4-epsilon)n log n) eventually for every epsilon>0. The argument does not assume h_F=1, does not assume that log h_F=o(n log n), and does not assert equality in (H2). A conjecture about real cyclotomic class numbers is unnecessary.

There is also a proof of (H2) that avoids Q<=2 altogether. Use Reg_K<=2^(d-1) Reg_F, Reg_F<=h_F Reg_F, and then (B1) and (B2). This yields the same lower bound. Thus even the auxiliary Kronecker argument is not load-bearing for the theorem under review.

## 5. Counting all small integral ideals

For x>=1, let

\[
A_K(x)=\#\{0\ne J\subseteq\mathcal O_K:N(J)\le x\}.
\]

At a real s>1, positivity of the ideal Dirichlet series gives

\[
A_K(x)\le x^s\zeta_K(s).
\tag{I1}
\]

For a rational prime p, write its distinct prime ideals in K as P_1,...,P_g, with residue degrees f_i. Since g<=n and f_i>=1, its Euler factor satisfies

\[
\prod_{i=1}^{g}(1-p^{-sf_i})^{-1}
\le(1-p^{-s})^{-g}\le(1-p^{-s})^{-n}.
\]

This also covers ramification: the identity sum_i e_i f_i=n still implies g<=n. Multiplying over p in the absolutely convergent region gives

\[
\zeta_K(s)\le\zeta(s)^n.
\tag{I2}
\]

Choosing the fixed value s=2, with no optimization or near-pole estimate needed,

\[
A_K(e^{cn})\le e^{2cn}\zeta(2)^n
=\exp\bigl((2c+\log\zeta(2))n\bigr).
\tag{I3}
\]

This estimate is uniform over all degree-n number fields. It counts every ideal, including multiple representatives of the same class, so

\[
|C_n(c)|\le A_K(e^{cn}).
\tag{I4}
\]

There is no missing injectivity assumption: choosing one small ideal in each represented class injects those classes into the finite set counted by A_K.

## 6. The exponent and its interpretation

Subtract (H2) from the logarithm of (I3)–(I4):

\[
\begin{aligned}
\log\frac{|C_n(c)|}{h_K}
&\le(2c+\log\zeta(2))n-\frac14 n\log n+o(n\log n)\\
&=-\frac14 n\log n+o(n\log n).
\end{aligned}
\]

The constant c is fixed, so its contribution is o(n log n). This proves (T), equivalently the frozen notation

\[
\frac{|C_n(c)|}{h_K}
\le\exp\bigl(-(1/4+o(1))n\log n\bigr).
\]

The set is nonempty, because the principal class has the representative R of norm 1. Hence the logarithm used above is defined. The proof supplies a vanishing fraction of classes admitting such representatives. It need not supply a matching lower bound on that fraction.

## 7. Exact scope of same-class scaling

Fix rho>0 and put c=rho log 2. Then e^(cn)=2^(rho n). The fraction above is eventually less than 1, so for every sufficiently large power of two n some ordinary ideal class has no integral representative of norm at most 2^(rho n).

If I is any nonzero fractional ideal and alpha is any nonzero element of K, then [alpha I]=[I]. Conversely, two fractional ideals represent the same ordinary ideal class exactly when one is a nonzero field-element multiple of the other. Thus an ideal in a class absent from C_n(c) cannot be scaled into an integral ideal with the required norm bound. Every class admits an integral ideal, so this obstruction applies even if inputs are restricted to integral ideals.

This is a feasibility obstruction to an algorithm that must produce such a scaled integral ideal for every input. It is independent of the algorithm's computational power, and randomness cannot produce a nonexistent valid output for a forbidden class. The counting fraction refers to uniformly counted ideal classes; it supplies no hardness or input-distribution assertion.

Integrality of the output is essential. Without it, multiplication by a rational inverse integer can make the fractional-ideal norm arbitrarily small. The result also does not constrain class-changing transformations, extension or restriction of fields, dimension changes, oracle-assisted transformations outside this specification, or the original computational-hardness objective.

The threshold may depend on rho, or equivalently c, and on the desired slack in the exponent. The qualitative analytic asymptotics used here provide no numerical starting dimension. No conclusion about a specified finite cryptographic dimension follows from this derivation alone.

## 8. Finite hand checks and attempted falsification

No computational experiment was run. The following are exact hand checks and attacks on potentially overstrong interpretations.

1. **n=2.** K=Q(i), F=Q. The formulas give D_K=2^2=4, D_F=2/2=1, and relative discriminant norm 4. Both regulator ranks are zero and the regulator formula gives 1=1. Gaussian integer division gives h_K=1, so |C_2(c)|/h_K=1 for every c>0. This prevents reading the asymptotic result as an explicit strict bound valid at every n; it does not contradict (T).

2. **n=4.** K=Q(zeta_8), F=Q(sqrt(2)). The formulas give D_K=4^4=256 and D_F=4^2/2=8, and 256/8^2=4. As an optional check on the finite class count, Minkowski's bound is
   \[
   \frac{4!}{4^4}\left(\frac4\pi\right)^2\sqrt{256}
   =\frac{24}{\pi^2}<3.
   \]
   Every class therefore has an integral representative of norm 1 or 2. The prime above 2 is unique and principal, generated by 1-zeta_8 of norm 2. Hence h_K=1 and again the fraction is 1. Minkowski's theorem is used only in this optional finite check, not in the asymptotic proof.

3. **n=8 discriminants.** The formulas give D_K=2^24 and D_F=2^11, whose quotient D_K/D_F^2 is 4. This checks the real-field factor 1/2 and the relative-discriminant norm against another exact case.

4. **Unit norm and class multiplicity.** The ideal R of norm 1 is always present; therefore a purported argument giving zero represented classes would fail. Conversely, many small ideals may represent one class; only the upper bound |C|<=A_K is used.

5. **Degree growth and fixed constants.** Both degrees grow, but both degree/log-discriminant ratios tend to zero explicitly. The ideal-count estimate would no longer have the stated negligible c-contribution if one silently allowed arbitrary c depending on n. The frozen quantifier fixes c, so this attack does not apply.

No finite counterexample to the actual asymptotic theorem was found. Small examples cannot determine its effective threshold, and no such threshold is inferred.
