                                                     Abstract
           The succinct LWE (CRYPTO ’24) and decomposed LWE (CRYPTO ’25) assumptions are
       two recently introduced variants of the celebrated LWE problem, known to imply the exis-
       tence of numerous new cryptographic primitives.  Yet, the hardness of these problems has
       thus far remained poorly understood, since there is no known reduction to LWE.
           In this work, we study these lattice problems from a foundational perspective. Our main
       technical contribution is a worst-case-to-average-case reduction fromsuperlattice SVP(Su-
       perSVP) to succinct SIS. We introduce SuperSVP as a variant of SVP for general lattices in
       which the solver is additionally given, as a hint, a short basis for a related superlattice. Com-
       bining this result with our quantum reduction from succinct SIS to succinct LWE, which is
       an analog of Regev’s SIS-to-LWE reduction, and with our search-to-decision reduction estab-
       lishes worst-case hardness for succinct LWE. Finally, we show that succinct and decomposed
       LWE are equivalent, extending this conclusion to decomposed LWE.
           Overall,  our work places succinct and decomposed LWE on firmer theoretical ground
       and, as a direct consequence, establishes theworst-casehardness of a variety of cryptographic
       primitives, known to be implied by these assumptions.

1    Introduction

The Learning With Errors (LWE) problem is the pillar of lattice-based cryptography and one of
the foundations of post-quantum cryptography, due to its conjectured hardness against quantum
algorithms.  The (decisional) LWE assumption asserts that a noisy linear encodings          TA+e       T is
computationally indistinguishable from a uniformly random vector even when the matrixAis
public. Due to its rich algebraic structure, the LWE problem enables fundamental cryptographic
primitives, such as fully homomorphic encryption [Gen09,vDGHV10,BV11,BGV12,GSW13],
attribute-based encryption [GVW13,BGG   +14], predicate encryption [GVW15], or classical veri-
fication of quantum computation [Mah18], to mention a few.
    Nevertheless, in the current state of affairs, not all cryptographic primitives are known to
be reducible from LWE. Motivated both by the construction of advanced cryptographic primi-
tives and by the goal of improving the concrete efficiency of existing schemes, the research com-
munity has recently investigated numerousnew variantsof the SIS/LWE problem and related
lattice assumptions, e.g., [ACL+22,AKSY22,BLNS23,CLM23,WW23b,FMN24,ARS24,Wee24,
AMR25, ABCR26].  This proliferation has also prompted community efforts to catalog the re-
sulting assumptions and clarify the reductions, generalizations, and other relationships among
them.  Examples include the ongoing Lattice Assumption Zoo project [LatZoo26] and the 2026
ICMS workshopNovel Lattice Assumptions[ICMS26].
    Among these assumptions, succinct LWE [Wee24] and decomposed LWE [AMR25], together
with their SIS counterparts [WW23a, Wee24, AMR26a], have enabled a growing collection of
constructions.  Examples include compact attribute-based encryption [Wee24,Wee25,AMR25,
LZF26], distributed and adaptively secure broadcast encryption [CW24,CHW25,WW25,GY26c],
flexible  and  identity-based  broadcast  encryption  [GY26a],  registered  attribute-based  encryp-
tion  [AMR25, CHW25, WW25],  laconic  function  evaluation  [Wee24, AMR25],  and  succinct
randomized  encodings  and  garbled  circuits  [AMR25, LLL26].    Further  applications  include
distributed  monotone-policy  encryption  [CW26a, AGY26a, CW26b, AGY26b, GY26b],  silent
threshold encryption and bounded-collusion registered functional encryption [CWY26], succinct
computational secret sharing [CW26b], secure group messaging [BBD     +26], functional commit-
ments  [WW23a],  and  correlation-intractable  hash  functions  for  batched  relations  [AMR26a].
Under additional assumptions, succinct LWE also supports extensions of registered ABE with
certified deletion and everlasting security [MSM25]. Yet, the hardness of these problems has thus
far remained opaque. Given their usefulness in the realm of cryptography, an outstanding open
question is to relate their difficulty to regular lattice problems and to shed some light on where
such hardness may come from.
    The objective of this work is to understand and solidify the foundations of decomposed LWE
and succinct LWE: We investigate how these assumptions relate to one another, how variants of
these assumptions connect, and which worst-case lattice problems can support their hardness.
In more detail, we show that (1) the (average-case) succinct SIS problem is at least as hard as a
worst-caseshortest vector problem over general lattices; (2) succinct and decomposed LWE are
equivalent and their search and decision variants are also equivalent; (3) succinct SIS reduces to
succinct LWE with a quantum reduction. Before stating our results more precisely, we recall the
definitions of succinct and decomposed LWE and review their common structure, through the
lens ofhintedlattice problems.

4    A Worst-Case to Average-Case Reduction for Succinct SIS

We prove the reduction described in the technical overview. A succinct SIS solver first gives one
short vector ofΛthat lies outside any fixed proper subspace with probability at least an inverse
polynomial in𝜆. Repeating this step gives𝑛independent vectors and then a shorter basis of the
full lattice. Iterating the basis improvement proves the worst-case reduction.
    All dimensions and input bit lengths are polynomially bounded in the security parameter𝜆,
and𝑛=𝜔(log𝜆).  Supplied scalar parameters are rational, with binary numerator/denominator
encodings  of  polynomial  length.   We  use  a  supplied  inverse-polynomial  lower  bound  on  the
solver’s success to set the maximum number of trials in each solver repetition.  Gaussian sam-
pling, numerical approximation, and failure to obtain full row rank within𝜆attempts contribute
a negligible error per invocation. The proofs first analyze exact initial Gaussian samples and then
include this error.
